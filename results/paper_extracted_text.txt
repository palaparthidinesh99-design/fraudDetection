ABSTRACT Credit card fraud detection presents a persistent challenge due to severe class imbalance and
the high costs associated with undetected fraudulent transactions. While oversampling techniques such
as Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) and Adaptive Synthetic Sampling Approach
for Imbalanced Learning (ADASYN) have improved minority class detection, most methods are cost-
agnostic and do not account for the financial impact of individual misclassifications. This paper proposes a
comprehensive framework for cost-sensitive oversampling, wherein the probability of generating synthetic
minority samples is proportional to the transaction amount, thereby prioritizing high-impact fraud cases.
We systematically evaluate standard and cost-sensitive variants of several popular oversampling methods—
including SMOTE, ADASYN, Borderline-SMOTE, SMOTE-ENN, and SMOTE-Tomek—across five
widely used machine learning classifiers Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, multi-layer perceptron, and
logistic regression. Experiments conducted on a real-world credit card dataset demonstrate that cost-sensitive
oversampling substantially increases total cost savings and reduces the average cost per misclassification,
with especially marked improvements for neural and linear models. The findings offer practical guidance
for deploying cost-sensitive solutions in imbalanced, high-stakes financial applications.
INDEX TERMS Credit card fraud detection, cost-sensitive learning, imbalanced classification, oversam-
pling, SMOTE, ADASYN, total cost savings.

and undermining consumer trust. Global fraud losses are
estimated to reach billions of dollars annually, imposing
heavy burdens on both financial institutions and individual
customers [4]. Consequently, there is a persistent demand
for highly effective and adaptive fraud detection systems

Our contributions are summarized as follows
• We introduce a general cost-sensitive oversampling
framework in which the number of synthetic minority
samples generated for each fraud instance is explicitly
proportional to its normalized misclassification cost.
This unified approach can be flexibly applied to a broad
family of oversampling algorithms, thereby prioritizing
high-value fraud cases during sample generation.
• We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of both standard
and cost-sensitive variants of popular oversampling
methods across multiple state-of-the-art ML classifiers.
• We provide extensive empirical evidence that cost-
sensitive oversampling substantially improves business-
relevant outcomes, such as total cost savings and average
cost per misclassification.
Summarily, our primary contribution is a general and
flexible cost-sensitive oversampling framework that can be
applied to a broad family of existing algorithms (e.g.,
SMOTE, ADASYN, Borderline-SMOTE), rather than being
a single, fixed method. This framework directly integrates
instance-dependent costs into the sample generation prob-
ability, a approach distinct from post-hoc cost-sensitive
learning or algorithm-specific modifications. We demon-
strate that this method systematically surpasses standard,
cost-agnostic oversampling by prioritizing high-impact fraud
cases, leading to superior financial outcomes across multiple
state-of-the-art classifiers.

II. RELATED WORKS
The constraints posed by class imbalance in credit card fraud
detection have prompted extensive research on resampling
and cost-sensitive methods. Early work identified that tradi-
tional metrics like accuracy are inadequate in such settings,
motivating a shift towards cost-aware evaluation [11].
A. OVERSAMPLING TECHNIQUES FOR IMBALANCED
FRAUD DATA
Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE)
remains the most widely adopted method for generat-
ing realistic synthetic minority examples [7]. Extensions
such as Borderline-SMOTE and ADASYN improve upon
SMOTE by focusing on hard-to-classify or borderline
samples [8]. Hybrid sampling techniques, e.g., SMOTE
combined with Tomek Links (SMOTE–Tomek) or Edited
Nearest Neighbours (SMOTE–ENN), have demonstrated
improved classifier performance by eliminating noisy or
redundant instances [12], and specifically in fraud-detection
contexts [13]. Moreover, Fonseca et al. [14] introduced k-
means SMOTE, which leverages clustering to enhance
sample diversity, while Dal Pozzolo et al. [15] conducted
a comprehensive evaluation of resampling methods on real
credit card fraud data, highlighting the practical relevance of
these techniques.
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have also
been explored for fraud oversampling. Conditional GAN-
based methods effectively generate synthetic fraudulent
transactions, enhancing detector performance over SMOTE
alone [16], [17]. For instance, Du et al. [18] proposed
AE-XGB-SMOTE-CGAN, a two-stage pipeline combining
SMOTE and GANs, yielding significant improvements
in both AUC and MCC. In addition, Fiore et al. [19]
showed that Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)-based
oversampling can also provide competitive gains, further
enriching the toolkit for data-level interventions.

Cost-sensitive learning addresses imbalanced classification
by embedding misclassification costs into either sampling

customize weights per example; Höppner et al. demonstrated
improved transfer-fraud detection using sample-level cost
adjustments [9]. Example-dependent cost-sensitive SMOTE
integrated with Bayes Minimum Risk further showed efficacy
in real fraud and credit scoring applications [10]. Additional
algorithm-level approaches include cost-sensitive decision
trees, boosted ensembles, and modified SVMs. For example,
cost-sensitive weak learners and bagged random forests have
effectively reduced misclassification cost [20], while Elshaar
and Sadaoui [21] combined cost-sensitive semi-supervised

Furthermore, Bahnsen et al. [22] developed an example-
dependent cost-sensitive logistic regression, demonstrating
that directly optimizing for financial cost leads to improved
business outcomes in fraud detection. More recently,
Olowookere and Adewale [23] proposed an active learning
framework incorporating cost-sensitive uncertainty and meta-
learning ensemble for credit card fraud detection, underlining
the increasing attention to economic considerations in model
design.
Despite many promising techniques, a comprehensive
comparison of cost-sensitive variants of oversampling
methods across several modern machine learning models
is missing. Most prior studies focus on single algo-
rithms or individual oversampling techniques; few evaluate
cost-sensitive forms of Borderline-SMOTE, SMOTE-ENN,
SMOTE-Tomek, GAN-based oversampling, and Majority-
Weighted Minority Oversampling in a unified framework.
Furthermore, evaluation often relies solely on statistical
metrics (e.g., F1, AUC) instead of incorporating cost-based
metrics such as monetary savings or net cost [24], [25].
Our work aims to fill these gaps by systematically
evaluating cost-sensitive adaptations of key oversampling
strategies in conjunction with multiple state-of-the-art clas-
sifiers, using both conventional and cost-sensitive evaluation
metrics, and by providing practical insights for their real-
world deployment in credit card fraud detection. It is
important to distinguish data-level approaches like ours
from algorithm-level cost-sensitive methods. The latter
embed costs directly into the classifier’s loss function [21]
or decision threshold [10]. While highly effective, these
approaches are often model-specific. In contrast, our data-
level framework is classifier-agnostic, allowing any standard
ML model to benefit from cost-sensitive learning without
internal modification, thus offering greater flexibility and
ease of implementation.
III. MATERIALS AND METHODS
This section details the methodologies employed for
addressing class imbalance in credit card fraud detection.
We describe the suite of oversampling techniques adapted
for this study, each aimed at improving the representation
of the minority (fraudulent) class in the training data.
Mathematical formulations are provided to clarify the
mechanisms underlying each approach. The subsequent
subsections outline the machine learning classifiers utilized,
followed by the performance evaluation metrics.
A. DATASET DESCRIPTION
This study uses the European credit card fraud dataset,
a widely recognized benchmark introduced by Dal
Pozzolo et al. [26]. The dataset consists of 284,807 transac-
tions recorded over two days in September 2013, of which
only 492 are labeled as fraud. Each transaction is represented
by 28 anonymized principal components (V1–V28), along
with the original Amount (transaction value in Euros) and
Time (elapsed seconds from the first transaction). The target
label, Class, is binary 1 for fraudulent and 0 for legitimate
transactions, yielding a highly imbalanced scenario typical of
real-world fraud detection.

framework for credit card fraud detection. The central
innovation is to integrate instance-level misclassification

most financially impactful fraud cases are more likely
to be synthetically represented in the training set. The
method operates entirely at the data level, maintaining full
compatibility with a broad range of supervised machine
learning classifiers.

Traditional oversampling methods treat all fraud cases
equally, regardless of the financial impact of individual trans-
actions. However, in real-world credit card fraud detection,
missing a high-value fraudulent transaction can result in
disproportionately large losses compared to low-value cases.
To address this, our method prioritizes the generation of
synthetic samples for high-cost frauds, ensuring that the
training set better reflects the economic risks present in
operational environments.

A. INTEGRATION WITH CLASSIFIERS
The resulting cost-sensitive resampled dataset D′ is used to
train any classifier of choice—Random Forest, XGBoost,
LightGBM, MLP , and Logistic Regression in this study—
without any modification to the original model or its loss
function. This preserves modularity and ease of imple-
mentation. Figure 1 presents a schematic overview of the
proposed pipeline, starting from cost assignment, through
cost-weighted oversampling, to downstream model training
and evaluation. This modular architecture allows for seamless
integration of various oversampling strategies and classifiers,
ensuring adaptability across different fraud detection envi-
ronments and supporting the goal of minimizing real-world
financial losses.
V. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Experiments were conducted using the European credit card
fraud dataset containing 284,807 transactions, with only
492 labeled as fraudulent. All features were standardized,
and the data was split 70/30 into training and test sets
while maintaining class imbalance. For cost-sensitive over-
sampling, each fraud instance’s misclassification cost was
set to its transaction amount, and the cost of false positives
was considered negligible. To ensure a fair and controlled
comparison, all classifiers were trained using the same
fixed set of hyperparameters, as detailed in Table 1, across
all oversampling techniques. No further hyperparameter
optimization was performed, allowing us to isolate the
effect of the oversampling methods themselves and for
reproducibility.
The number of synthetic samples generated by each
oversampler matched the number of majority class examples
in the training set. To achieve a perfectly balanced training
set, the oversampling ratio was set such that the total
number of synthetic minority samples generated was equal
to the number of majority class examples in the training
subset. This means the target was to bring the minority
class count to 100% of the majority class count, resulting
in a 1:1 class ratio post-oversampling. Cost-sensitive variants
reweighted sample generation based on transaction amount as
described in Section IV. Model performance was evaluated
by Accuracy, Specificity, Sensitivity, F1-Score, Total Cost
Savings (TCS), Average Cost per Misclassification (ACM),
and Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC). Each experiment
was repeated three times and mean results are reported. The
random state parameter was fixed across all experiments to
ensure reproducibility of results, allowing identical data splits
and consistent random initialization during oversampling and
model training. This facilitates fair cross-model comparison.
B. RESUL TS
Table 2 presents a comprehensive comparison of the classi-
fication performance for all models and oversampling tech-
niques. Across the board, accuracy and specificity remained
exceptionally high for every method and classifier, reflecting
the dataset’s class imbalance and the ease with which legit-
imate transactions are correctly identified. However, more
telling differences emerged when focusing on sensitivity,
F1-score, and especially the cost-sensitive metrics—total
cost savings (TCS) and average cost per misclassification
(ACM).
A close inspection reveals that for Random Forest and
XGBoost, cost-sensitive variants of Borderline-SMOTE and
SMOTE-ENN improved the F1-score and led to some
of the highest total cost savings, even when sensitivity
was not always maximized. For example, in Random
Forest, cost-sensitive Borderline-SMOTE produced both the
highest F-measure and the lowest ACM, while the standard
SMOTE-ENN had the highest sensitivity, demonstrating that
maximizing recall does not always result in optimal cost
efficiency. Similarly, in XGBoost, cost-sensitive SMOTE-
ENN and standard SMOTE-ENN alternated in producing the
best cost savings and sensitivity, respectively, highlighting
that targeting high-value frauds offers distinct advantages for
financial outcomes.
LightGBM’s performance exhibited greater sensitivity
to the oversampling method applied. While the highest
sensitivity was achieved with standard SMOTE-Tomek,
cost-sensitive SMOTE-ENN and ADASYN consistently
produced higher F-measure and superior financial metrics.
This pattern continued with the MLP , where cost-sensitive
SMOTE-Tomek provided the best total cost savings and
the lowest ACM, but cost-sensitive ADASYN achieved the
top sensitivity. In both MLP and LightGBM, integrating
cost information into the oversampling process resulted in
models that more efficiently prioritized and detected high-
value frauds.
Interestingly, for Logistic Regression—a comparatively
simple classifier—cost-sensitive oversampling variants deliv-
ered substantial gains across all major metrics. Sensitivity
reached 0.865 for all cost-sensitive methods, and total
cost savings and ACM were also consistently highest and
lowest, respectively, for these approaches. This indicates
that even linear models, which may not capture complex
data patterns as deeply as tree ensembles or neural net-
works, can nonetheless benefit from cost-weighted sample
generation when applied to highly imbalanced, cost-sensitive
problems. For instance, Cost-sensitive SMOTE-ENN with
XGBoost achieved a Total Cost Savings (TCS) of 12,980.10,
which is an improvement over the standard SMOTE-
ENN (TCS = 12,433.85) and provides a clear financial
benefit.
The ROC analysis, depicted in Figures 3–7, further
illustrates these patterns. While AUC values were generally
high for all models, cost-sensitive Borderline-SMOTE and
SMOTE-ENN sometimes delivered modest but consistent
improvements, particularly for LightGBM and MLP . These
small but meaningful gains reinforce the business value of
cost-sensitive approaches, which may not always yield the
highest recall but result in better financial performance for
the institution.

C. DISCUSSION
Taken together, these results provide compelling evidence
that cost-sensitive oversampling mechanisms offer tangible
benefits over traditional, cost-agnostic resampling in credit
card fraud detection. Particularly in metrics aligned with real-
world impact—such as total cost savings and average cost
per misclassification—cost-sensitive methods that directly
incorporate transaction value into the sample weighting
process enable models to better prioritize high-impact frauds.
It is important to note that no single method universally
dominated all metrics or classifiers. In some cases, the highest

to a wide variety of models and cost structures, offers a
principled approach for practitioners aiming to reduce real-
world losses in imbalanced, high-stakes domains.
VI. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This study introduced and benchmarked a general frame-
work for cost-sensitive oversampling in credit card fraud
detection, rigorously comparing classical and modern tech-
niques across several machine learning models. The results
demonstrate that by integrating transaction-level costs into
the resampling process, it is possible to achieve substan-
tial gains in business-relevant outcomes—most notably,
total cost savings and reduction in the average cost
per misclassification—regardless of the underlying classi-
fier. Cost-sensitive Borderline-SMOTE, SMOTE-ENN, and
SMOTE-Tomek emerged as particularly effective, especially
in neural and linear models.
Future research should, in addition to an ablation study,
investigate adaptive or dynamic cost matrices, the application
of deep generative models, and extension to multiclass or
streaming data scenarios. Most importantly, large-scale, real-
time deployment of cost-sensitive oversampling frameworks
in financial institutions will provide further validation of their
practical utility and impact.
Furthermore, the application of oversampling inherently
increases the size of the training dataset, which can elevate
computational demands during model training. This could
be a limiting factor in real-time or resource-constrained
environments. A promising direction for future work is to
explore techniques that reduce this computational overhead.
Inspired by methods used in other domains, such as optimiz-
ing transform hop sizes in signal processing [44] or efficient
input representation [45], future research could investigate
strategic sub-sampling of the generated synthetic data or
the development of more efficient, selective oversampling
algorithms that maintain financial performance gains while
minimizing added computational complexity.
